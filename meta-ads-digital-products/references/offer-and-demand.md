# Offer and demand validation

Use this guide before recommending campaign spend when the product, buyer, price, or reason to buy is unclear.

## Define the offer in buyer terms

Write down:

- **Buyer:** a reachable group with a shared job, context, and buying trigger—not “everyone who likes productivity.”
- **Problem or progress:** what the buyer is trying to do, avoid, or accomplish now.
- **Product:** what the customer receives, how it works, compatibility, access period, support, and meaningful limits.
- **Offer and terms:** price, billing cadence, trial, guarantee/refund policy, payment plan, license, and fulfillment timing. Use only approved terms.
- **Reason to choose it:** product experience, method, convenience, quality, or evidence that is real and relevant.
- **Proof:** customer evidence with permission, product demo, credentials, public documentation, or a clear description of what remains unproven.

An ad hook is not the offer. A discount or urgency line cannot repair unclear product value, poor fit, or missing trust.

## Choose what to sell first

When the seller has multiple products, compare each candidate on buyer/problem clarity, observed demand, net contribution per order or customer, strength of the purchase path, proof and demo availability, fulfillment/support readiness, and fit with the seller's cash risk. Choose one lead offer for the first paid test and state why it is the best-supported candidate. Do not choose only because it is the cheapest, has the most clicks, or has the largest theoretical lifetime value.

Keep the offer stable while learning whether the chosen buyer, message, and path can produce paid customers. Test a second product after the first has a clear result or when a separate test has enough budget and tracking to answer its own question. If no offer is ready, identify the smallest useful validation step rather than spreading a small budget across the catalog.

For courses, communities, or other considered purchases, a lead, webinar, demo, or trial path can be appropriate when it resolves real purchase friction and the seller can follow up reliably. Define how the path reaches paid, retained contribution; lead volume or cost per signup is an intermediate signal, not proof of acquisition success.

## Assess evidence before buying traffic

Separate evidence by strength and scope. User interviews and support logs can reveal language and objections; page, checkout, and payment data can show behavior; paid conversions and retained customers can show that some buyers completed the path. None alone proves every market segment will respond to ads.

Record the evidence source, date range, segment, sample size, and limitation. Treat competitor ads, public case studies, platform recommendations, and creator anecdotes as context or hypotheses—not as this seller's forecast.

If evidence is weak, propose the smallest useful validation step: interview target buyers, clarify product demo/preview, test checkout usability, validate price and terms, or run a small paid test only if the loss is affordable. Do not dress an unvalidated promise as a proven result.

## Readiness verdict

Use one of these:

- **Ready for a capped sales test:** buyer, offer, purchase path, economics, event source, and capacity are sufficiently clear; name any remaining assumptions.
- **Revise before ads:** a specific offer, trust, fulfillment, measurement, or checkout issue should be addressed first.
- **Insufficient evidence:** demand, buyer, price, economics, or product readiness is too uncertain to select a responsible test. Give one concrete evidence-gathering next step.

Do not use a numeric readiness score as the probability of success. State which evidence would change the verdict.
